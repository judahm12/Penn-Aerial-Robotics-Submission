## Writeup
Step 1:
I began step 1 by observing how the shapes differ from the background. I found that the shapes are solid colors, while the texture of the grass changes rapidly. I first made a mask to detect grass, and I iterated through known patches of grass to collect HSV data. In looking at the mean and variance of hue, saturation, and value for the grass data, I found that hue was lower variance than saturation and value, so I used hue as the primary signal to differentiate between grass and shapes. This successfully detected all of the shapes except the circle and the triangle, which share a similar hue to the grass. I realized I could use the grass’s high saturation variance to my advantage, and I created a second mask based on local saturation variance. 
After plotting the initial mask, I found that there was some noise left in the background, so I applied the opening algorithm to delete the erroneous blobs and fill back the holes that were formed in the shapes. My final mask included pixels that were either not grass, or labeled as grass but had low saturation variance—intended to rescue the green and yellow shapes. Then, I computed the contours and filtered the contours to be greater than an area threshold. Lastly, I filtered the points within the contours to discard vertices that lay within epsilon of the chord drawn between their neighbors. This allowed me to preserve only the corners of shapes. Finally, I calculated the centroids and added text.

Step 2:
	My algorithm from step 1 worked quite well on the video format of step 2. The changes I made to the algorithm were minor. The main issue I had to solve was false detection of shapes on grass patches near the edge. When windows hang off of the edge of the image, OpenCV replicates edge pixels which can give deceivingly low variance values. This makes blobs near the edge appear artificially smooth. By simply increasing the area threshold of the contours, I was able to circumvent this issue.

Step 3:
	Step 3 was what undoubtedly took the most effort. I began step 3 by sampling HSV data from several frames and accumulating a histogram for each channel. I figured that since the background occupied the majority of the frame, there would be clear splits in the histogram. I was wrong because gravel varies widely in brightness and the shapes’ own gradients spread them across various hue bins. I calculated Otsu by hand, iterating through each possible threshold to maximize the mean difference squared, multiplied by the fraction of points in each of the two groups. Although I initially used an open_cv function to compute Otsu, after calculating by hand I realized that Otsu is maximized when the amount of data in both groups is split evenly. Since the shapes only make up 5-10% of the data, Otsu was splitting the background against itself, lumping background pixels into the shape data. Next, I tried edge detection. I blurred the image to suppress noise, and used Canny to find edges. However, the background contains thousands of edges, so not even blur can prevent such false positives. 

I realized that I needed to focus on developing a background-agnostic algorithm that focuses more on the difference between local standard deviation in the background and the internally homogenous shapes. I converted the image to greyscale because I wanted to measure spatial variation instead of hue. I then used box filters to calculate local standard deviation for each pixel in the image. Local standard deviation finds variance in small windows, but it's possible for smooth background patches to escape. To circumvent this, I blurred the local_sd array so that if there is texture anywhere in a larger window it would be picked up. It’s important to note that you can’t simply increase the window size of the local_sd kernel because the color gradient of the shapes is such that it risks raising the SD of the shape pixels to be closer to the background SD. As you increase window size, the SD of shape pixels increases because the color continues changing, but the background SD stays roughly constant.

Next, it was time to pass the SD values through a threshold. Due to Otsu’s failure, I searched for a new method of thresholding. Before blurring, I first capped local SD values to be no more than ~ 1 sigma above the median. This is because I found that stark internal color boundaries were causing larger spikes in SD than background noise, so I wanted to ensure that these unintended spikes are able to be blurred out in future steps. Then, I calculated the threshold for blurred SD to be the median - 4 * sigma. I chose to use median and median absolute deviation from the median (MAD) instead of mean and standard deviation to make my statistics more robust to outliers. I wanted the threshold to be based on the SD values of the background, and resistant to spikes in SD caused by the color gradients. By construction, half of the data lies within one MAD of the median. This interval is the IQR, so in a normal distribution this equates to roughly 0.6745σ. So, σ ≈ 1.4826 × MAD. By applying this factor to MAD, I was able to make the threshold interpretable in terms of SD. After filtering the data to find all pixels with blurred SD more than 4 sigma below the median, I applied morphology to the mask. 

I cleaned the mask by applying opening and then closing. Opening (erode then dilate) removes isolated white specks from the background. Closing (dilate then erode) fills in the holes of shapes. It’s important to note that the asymmetric sizing of the opening and closing kernels was intentional. Any blob smaller than the opening kernel is erased. Since the white specks are usually small, it’s better to err on the side of caution when choosing the radius of the opening kernel than to risk losing edges of the shapes. However, closing doesn’t carry the same risks because its primary role is to add pixels, so we can afford to use a larger kernel for closing. After opening and closing, I filtered the blobs by area. I chose to do this before dilating the blobs in order to avoid dilating non-shape blobs and failing to differentiate them from true shapes. Since the measurement of variance is computed over a window, a pixel is only considered smooth if all of its surrounding pixels are as well. The detected boundary therefore sits inside the true boundary by a roughly constant width, and dilation adds that band back.

The final change I made to the background-agnostic algorithm was to add a high-pass filter to improve the contour for the trapezoid. Since the trapezoid’s color gradient is spatially large and stark, my initial algorithm failed to detect the edges of the trapezoid. Despite capping the local SD values, trapezoid’s gradient is wide enough to fit entirely inside the 31-pixel wide pooling window for the blur step, causing the values to stay near background level. So, I harnessed the difference between the trapezoid gradient and the background in the speed that their intensity varied. While the gravel’s intensity varied in small 3 pixel wide segments, the trapezoid’s gradient changed over 10-15 pixels. Since the shape’s color gradient varies roughly linearly, blurring the gradient segments will return an average approximately equal to the original pixel value. On the other hand, background speckles aren’t consistently equal to the background average. Thus, subtracting the blurred grayscale from the original grayscale allowed me to improve the contours of the trapezoid.

Limitations:
My method assumes that the shapes are smoother than the background. If this is not the case, an alternative approach to find the background would be to use geometry to find which component is most often touching the border of the image. However, because my method chooses the threshold to be 4 sigma below the median, my algorithm won’t select any shapes, rather than inverting the labels.  Additionally, the trapezoid is an example of an edge case for my algorithm. When an object’s color gradient is wider than the pooling window, it makes it difficult to differentiate between background noise and a true gradient. Furthermore, when objects pass over each other in the video, my approach fails to distinguish between the two overlapping shapes. To circumvent this problem, my first step would be to filter non-convex shapes based on where color changes most rapidly. Note that all of the shapes begin as convex, but when they overlap they become non-convex. Lastly, a shape drifting off screen still incorrectly reports a centroid and radius, which interferes with depth estimation. 

Part 4:
	For a camera, apparent size is inversely proportional to distance. If we have a circle of known radius, we can use similar triangles to calculate the distance from the camera to the shape. The given matrix has cx=cy=0 (which would put the optical axis in the corner of the image), so I instead assumed the principal point to be at the center of the image. Fx and fy differ by a small amount, so I used their geometric mean, because a circle projects to an ellipse whose area depends on the product of fx and fy.

Since the circle is the only shape with known size, I iterate through each shape to find the most circular one. Calculating the horizontal position uses the same ratio as the depth formula. I assume that the other four shapes are on the same plane as the circle, and thus share its depth values. Altogether, this gives a depth of roughly 6.5 m.
